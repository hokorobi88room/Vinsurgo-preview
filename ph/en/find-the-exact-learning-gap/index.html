When a Lesson Leaves You Saying ‘I Still Don’t Get It’: Find the Exact Gap

Separate an unclear meaning, a missing link in the reasoning, and the limit of a rule before you go back through the whole lesson.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 8/31/2026 Updated 8/31/2026 Information checked 8/30/2026 7 min read

This article is for you if

You can follow a lesson, module, or tutorial while it is open, but later you cannot tell which part you actually understood and which part is still missing.

By the end, you will be able to

You will be able to tell whether you are missing a meaning, a connection, or a limit, then choose a smaller question instead of restarting the whole topic.

The module looked clear on your phone. Why is one part suddenly impossible to explain now?

Maybe you watched part of a lesson while waiting for a jeepney, reviewed notes after class, or opened a tutorial again at home after work.

While the screen was open, the steps seemed to hold together. When you put the phone down and try to explain the idea in your own words, the thread breaks.

One key word may still be unclear. You may know both steps but not the reason between them. Or the rule may work in the example even though you do not know where it stops working.

All three can feel like the same problem.

They need different questions.

Let’s find the gap before we restart the lesson

  • Let’s see whether one key word still has no clear meaning in our own words.
  • Let’s see whether we can explain the reason that joins two steps.
  • Let’s see what condition would make the rule stop working in the usual way.

1. Ask whether the key word means anything clear without the lesson beside you

Sometimes the problem is not the whole topic. One important term may still be only a familiar label.

You might recognise it from a school module, a review video, or training material. But if you have to explain it without repeating the same term, the meaning may suddenly become thin.

We can call this a meaning gap: the word looks familiar, but the idea behind it is still blurry.

Rozenblit and Keil studied a related effect called the illusion of explanatory depth. In simple terms, people can feel that they understand how something works in more detail than they can actually explain. Their 2002 paper examined explanations of familiar systems and devices, not online courses, so it does not show that every unclear lesson comes from this effect. It does show why trying to explain an idea can expose limits that simple familiarity can hide. (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002)

Try one small check:

Can I explain this important word without using the same word again?

Imagine you are reviewing percentage change on your phone after class while waiting at a jeepney terminal for the trip home.

You can follow the formula, yet “percentage change” still means little more than “the percentage I get at the end.”

Put it into ordinary words:

Percentage change tells me how large a change is compared with where I started.

Now the term has a simple picture behind it: a starting point, a movement away from it, and a comparison between the two.

You have not finished the whole topic. But you have found one precise thing that needs clearer meaning.

2. Ask whether the missing part is the reason between two steps

Another kind of confusion appears when the important words make sense but the reasoning breaks in the middle.

You understand the first step and the next step separately. What is missing is the reason that connects them.

We can call this a connection gap.

A 1994 study by Michelene Chi and colleagues gives a useful reason to test this kind of gap. 14 eighth-grade students were prompted to explain each line of a text about the circulatory system to themselves, while 10 students in a comparison group read the same text twice without those prompts. The prompted group showed a larger gain from pretest to posttest on the researchers’ measures of understanding. The study was small and focused on one subject, so it does not prove that self-explanation always improves learning. It supports the narrower idea that producing an explanation can help reveal and build links that reading alone may leave weak. (Chi et al., 1994)

Suppose you are checking a spreadsheet for a class fundraiser or a small barangay youth activity. One value rises from 80 to 100.

The increase itself is 20. You also remember that the formula uses the starting figure, so the next calculation is 20 divided by 80.

Now stop at the step that is easy to copy without understanding:

Why 80?

If the only answer you have is “because that is the formula,” you have located the missing link.

The reason is:

We compare the size of the change with the value we started from.

Twenty divided by 80 is 0.25, so the increase is 25%.

The calculation now answers a clear question. The rise of 20 is one quarter of the starting value.

Your next search no longer has to be “percentage change explained.” It can be the much smaller question:

Why does this step follow from the step before it?

3. Ask whether you know the rule only in examples where nothing goes wrong

A correct answer can still hide one last gap.

You may understand the term and the connection, but only in examples where every condition lets the rule work normally.

This can be a boundary gap. A boundary is the condition that changes when, or how, a rule can be used.

Keep the percentage-change example, but imagine you are testing a simple spreadsheet before using it to track online-selling orders or stock for a small sari-sari store.

This time, a value moves from 0 to 20.

The amount of change is still 20.

The usual formula asks how large the change is relative to the starting value. Here that means dividing 20 by 0, and division by zero is undefined.

For that reason, the ordinary formula does not give a normal percentage-change result from a starting value of zero.

You have not undone the earlier explanation. You have found a case that needs different treatment.

That gives you the third question:

What could I change in this example that would make the rule stop working in the usual way?

You do not need to hunt for every exception.

One clear edge can show whether you understand the rule itself rather than only the pattern of the examples you were given.

Three different gaps need three different fixes

These checks belong in this order because each one tests a different layer of understanding.

Start with meaning. If an important term is still blurry, the reasoning built on it will stay unstable.

Then check the connection. If the terms are clear but you cannot explain why one step leads to the next, more definitions may not repair the missing bridge.

Finally, test the limit. Once the meaning and the connection are clear, changing one condition can show whether you understand the rule beyond the example that introduced it.

This is a practical three-question check, not a scientific list of every possible learning problem. You may also have more than one kind of gap at the same time.

The useful shift is from one large sentence to three smaller ones:

“Which word is still unclear?”

“What reason connects these steps?”

“What condition makes this rule stop working normally?”

Each question gives you a more specific next move.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s choose one lesson that still feels fuzzy and explain its most important word in plain language.
  • Let’s place two steps beside each other and say the reason that links them.
  • Let’s change one condition in a tiny example and see whether the rule still behaves the same way.

You do not need to understand the whole subject at once.

We are only trying to name the next missing piece clearly enough that you know what to review, ask, or test.

Once the gap has a name, the next step can become much smaller.
