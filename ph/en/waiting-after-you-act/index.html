You Have Done Your Part: 3 Ways to Wait Without Chasing the Reply

Use a simple idea from Epictetus to see what still needs action from you, what now belongs to someone else, and what to learn when the answer comes.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 9/1/2026 Updated 9/1/2026 Information checked 8/29/2026 6 min read

This article is for you if

You have already done what you can for now, but you keep checking or replaying the situation because another person's answer still matters to you.

By the end, you will be able to

You will be able to separate your remaining action from another person's decision, finish one useful next step, and use the eventual result as information instead of a verdict on yourself.

We already pressed send. Why are we still carrying the reply around with us?

You leave work or school and check your phone while waiting for a jeepney or bus.

No reply.

You check again after you get home.

Still nothing.

Maybe you are waiting to hear about a job. Maybe you sent a proposal, asked someone for help, or apologised after a difficult conversation.

The answer matters, so silence can feel like a problem that is still asking you to work on it.

Your mind offers ideas.

Maybe I should explain again.

Maybe I should send one more message.

Maybe no reply already means no.

But an open situation is not always an open task.

In Elizabeth Carter’s English translation of Epictetus’s Enchiridion, section 1 begins:

“Some things are in our control and others not.”

The same passage includes “whatever are our own actions” among the things in our control, while reputation and command are placed outside that group. You can read the passage in the MIT Internet Classics Archive.

For everyday life, we can turn that old distinction into one simple question:

Is there still an action for me here, or am I waiting for someone else’s part?

Let’s stop treating every silence like more work

  • Let’s separate what we can still do from what another person must decide.
  • Let’s choose one next move that has a clear stopping point.
  • Let’s use the reply for learning when it arrives, without making it a verdict on ourselves.

1. Find the point where your part becomes someone else’s decision

Imagine you finish an online job interview at home in the afternoon.

You want the role, and that wish is not a problem.

The problem starts when “I want the job” quietly turns into “I need to make them choose me.”

Your part includes how you prepared, how clearly you answered, whether you promised to send anything, and what you can improve for the next interview.

Their part includes who else they meet, what the team needs, when they decide, and whom they finally choose.

Epictetus’s distinction helps because it separates your own action from what other people do. It does not say that outside results are unimportant; it says they are not yours to perform.

Try drawing a short control line:

Still mine

  • Send the document I promised.
  • Note the question that made me hesitate.
  • Prepare a clearer answer for the next suitable interview.

Not mine

  • The employer’s final choice.
  • The other applicants.
  • The time the reply arrives.
  • The opinion they form after the interview.

Suppose the call ended just before dinner, and your laptop is still open on the dining table. You write down one weak answer, close the notebook, and join the rest of your evening.

The decision is still open.

Your part, for now, may not be.

That difference keeps waiting from pretending to be productive effort.

2. Choose a next move that can end on your side

Now imagine the unanswered message is personal.

You sent an apology to a friend after a tense exchange in a group chat. By the time you are on the ride home, there is still no answer.

You may hope they forgive you.

But “make them forgive me” is not an action you can finish alone.

You can make sure the apology says what you are sorry for. You can correct something practical if it can still be corrected, then give the other person room to decide what they need next.

Your immediate action can be complete while the relationship is still unsettled.

The same method works after you send work to a client or colleague:

Result I hope for:
They accept my proposal.

What depends on them:
Whether they say yes.

What I can finish:
Check what I promised, send anything genuinely missing, and decide when I will look for a reply again.

That final choice creates a next check point. It is simply a later time when new information could give you a real decision to make.

Until then, opening email or chat again does not create another useful step.

You might put the phone down while you eat, talk with someone at home, or prepare what you need for tomorrow.

You are not acting as if the answer does not matter.

You are giving the waiting period an edge.

3. When the answer arrives, ask what it actually teaches you

A reply may arrive the next day while you are buying merienda or taking a short break.

It says no.

Before the disappointment turns into a full judgment of yourself, separate two questions:

What happened?

And:

What does this result tell me about the part that was mine?

A careful proposal can be rejected. A poorly prepared one can sometimes be accepted.

That is why the outcome matters but cannot measure the quality of every choice you made.

Instead, look for information that can change a future action.

If several people misunderstand the same line in your proposal, that line may need work. If several interviews expose the same missing skill, that repeated pattern may show you what to practise next.

The reply can still sting.

Return to the control line and ask whether the new information changes something you can do.

If it does, you now have a new action.

If it does not, there may be nothing more to solve today.

Disappointment can be real without becoming endless work.

Separate the roles first, finish your side next, then learn from the outcome

The three parts belong in this order. First, the control line keeps another person’s decision from becoming part of your workload; second, a finishable action gives your effort somewhere useful to go; third, the eventual result gives you information for what comes after.

Skip the first step and you may keep chasing a decision you cannot make. Skip the second and you may understand the boundary but still keep circling the situation; skip the third and you may miss feedback that deserves a change.

The aim is not to care less.

It is to care about the answer without taking responsibility for the part that never belonged to you.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s take one reply we are waiting for and divide the situation into Still mine and Not mine.
  • Let’s pick one useful step from the first side that can honestly reach an ending today.
  • Let’s decide when we will check for new information again, then return the time in between to the rest of our day.
