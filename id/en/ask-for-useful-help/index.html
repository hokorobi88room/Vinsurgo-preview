Stuck and Not Sure What to Ask? Use a 3-Part Help Brief

Tell the other person what you are trying to achieve, show the exact gap, and choose how far you want their help to go.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 02/09/2026 Updated 02/09/2026 Information checked 29/08/2026 6 min read

This article is for you if

You are stuck on a task and want clear help without handing the whole task to someone else.

By the end, you will be able to

You will be able to make a short help brief that states your goal, shows the gap clearly, and sets the amount of help you want.

You have opened the chat. How do we explain the problem without sending the whole story?

Maybe your class group is waiting for the final file, or a teammate has replied, “What exactly is wrong?”

You type a long message, delete part of it, then wonder whether the other person will still have to guess.

If the request is too broad, the helper has to search for the real problem. If you hand over everything, the task may be finished while the part that confused you stays unclear.

There is a middle path: make the request small enough to understand, but complete enough to answer.

The short version

  • Let’s name the result you are trying to get.
  • Let’s show what you expected, what happened, and what you already tried.
  • Let’s say whether you want a clue, an explanation, an example, or direct help finishing.

1. Begin with the result you want

When a task has been going wrong for a while, it is easy to start with every step that led there.

But the person helping you first needs to know where you are trying to arrive.

Imagine you are helping a campus event committee finish a shared slide deck. The chart looks fine while you edit it, but becomes hard to read after you export the final file for the committee group chat.

You could write:

“The slides are messed up and I have tried several things.”

Or you could start with:

“I’m trying to export the presentation so the chart stays readable in the final file.”

The second opening gives the other person a destination. It helps them separate useful details from background noise.

The same idea works in an office or small team:

“I’m trying to make this shared sheet show only this month’s entries.”

It also works when you are asking about a lesson:

“I want to understand why this method works, not only copy the final answer.”

You do not need to prove how much effort you have already spent. You only need to make the finish line visible.

A simple first line is:

“I’m trying to ______.”

Once the goal is clear, the rest of the question has a direction.

2. Show the gap: expected, happened, tried

A clear goal still leaves one question: what exactly is different from what you wanted?

Instead of describing only the frustration, you can put the gap where another person can see it.

Suppose you are preparing a monthly order recap in a shared spreadsheet for a small online shop team. You change the date filter before the team checks this month’s orders; the visible rows change, but the total at the top stays the same.

You can separate the problem into three short parts:

Expected: When I choose this month, I expected the total to change with the visible rows.
Happened: The rows change, but the total does not.
Tried: I refreshed the sheet and checked that the filter itself is working.

Now the helper has something specific to inspect instead of a general statement such as, “The total is wrong.”

Stack Overflow’s current Ask Wizard uses a similar structure for first-time question askers. It separates the problem details from what was tried and what results were expected; that tool is for programming questions, so we are borrowing the communication pattern rather than treating it as a rule for every kind of problem. (Stack Overflow Help Center)

The same pattern can help with a non-technical situation.

Imagine a colleague reads a slide you prepared for a weekly review.

Expected: I wanted the slide to make the main cause of the delay clear.
Happened: The reader understood a different cause.
Tried: I shortened the text, but the main reason is still easy to miss.

The question is not better because it sounds more formal. It is better because the difference is visible.

3. Decide how far you want the help to go

Even when the problem is clear, the other person can still give the wrong amount of help.

They may send you the finished answer when you wanted one hint, or give a long explanation when you only needed an urgent fix.

Picture yourself following an online coding lesson from your kos room or home after dinner. A friend offers to send the completed code, but you mainly want to understand the step where your version stops working.

You can ask for a clue:

“Can you point me to the first thing I should check?”

You can ask for an explanation:

“Can you explain why this part changes when I run it?”

You can ask for one worked example:

“Can you show me this one case, then let me try the next one?”

Or you can ask for direct help when finishing now matters more:

“I need to finish this today. Can you help me fix it now and explain the key step afterwards?”

These requests serve different needs. None is automatically the best one every time.

A 2011 paper on help-seeking in a geometry tutoring system discusses a distinction between help aimed at learning and help aimed mainly at completing a task. Across two studies, feedback about help-seeking improved students’ help-seeking behaviour while they used the system, and the second study found improved help-seeking behaviour on new geometry content during the following month after the special support had ended. The setting was specific, so it does not prove that one question format works for everyone; it supports the narrower point that help-seeking behaviour can change with practice and feedback in at least some learning settings. (Roll, Aleven, McLaren and Koedinger, 2011)

That difference matters in everyday life too. Sometimes you need another pair of hands, sometimes one missing explanation, and sometimes one example followed by space to continue alone.

A useful help request has a destination, evidence, and a boundary

The three parts work best in order. The goal gives the conversation a destination, the evidence shows the exact gap, and the help level sets the boundary between what the other person does and what you keep doing yourself.

Without the goal, the details can turn into a long story. Without evidence, the helper must guess. Without a boundary, you may receive a complete solution when you wanted to learn, or a lesson when you only needed a quick fix.

Put them together and your message can stay short:

“I’m trying to make this monthly total follow the date filter. The rows change but the total does not, and I have already refreshed the sheet and checked the filter. Can you explain what I should check next rather than fixing the whole file for me?”

It does not need to be a perfect question. It only needs to make the next useful step clear to both of you.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s make the gap visible with three lines: expected, happened, tried.
  • Let’s choose the kind of help that fits this moment before someone else takes over the task.
