After You Send It, What Is Still Yours to Do? 3 Steps for Waiting Without Chasing the Answer

Use a simple distinction from Epictetus to separate your next move from the reply, choice or decision that now belongs to someone else.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 01/09/2026 Updated 01/09/2026 Information checked 29/08/2026 6 min read

This article is for you if

You have sent the message, joined the interview, made the request or apologised, but you keep checking because the final answer now depends partly on someone else.

By the end, you will be able to

You will be able to separate what is still yours to do from what is not, finish one useful next action, and use the eventual result as information rather than a verdict on yourself.

The message is sent. Why are we still opening the chat as if the answer might change every minute?

You finish a video interview from your kos room in Bandung and close the laptop.

A little later, the phone lights up with a WhatsApp notification.

It is from another chat.

You put the phone down, but your attention stays beside it.

Maybe I should send a follow-up now.

Maybe my answer sounded weak.

Maybe no reply already means no.

The result matters, so waiting starts to feel like another job.

But a situation can still be open even when your part is finished for now.

In Elizabeth Carter’s English translation of Epictetus’s Enchiridion, section 1 begins:

“Some things are in our control and others not.”

In that passage, Carter translates our own actions as belonging to what is in our control, while reputation and command appear among what is not. The full section is available in the MIT Internet Classics Archive.

For an ordinary day, that old distinction gives us one useful question:

What part of this situation still needs an action from me?

Let’s give the waiting period a clear shape

  • Let’s separate the part we can still act on from the answer another person must give.
  • Let’s choose one useful step that has a real stopping point.
  • Let’s let the final result teach us something without using it as a verdict on ourselves.

1. Separate your part from the decision you cannot make

Imagine the video interview has just ended.

You want the role, but the sentence “I need them to choose me” joins together work that belongs to different people.

Your part includes how you prepared, how clearly you answered, whether you send a promised document, and what you decide to improve next.

Their part includes who else they speak with, what the team needs, when they decide, and whom they finally choose.

Epictetus’s distinction helps because it stops those two sides from becoming one blurred problem. His point is not that outside results do not matter; it is that another person’s action is not something you can perform for them.

Try drawing a simple control line after the interview:

Still mine

  • Send the document or detail I promised.
  • Note the question that exposed a weak part of my answer.
  • Practise a clearer answer before the next suitable interview.

Not mine

  • The recruiter’s final choice.
  • Who else is being considered.
  • The exact time the reply arrives.
  • The opinion they form after the conversation ends.

The second side can still matter to you. It simply does not become more controllable because you keep looking at the phone.

Picture the fan turning in the room while the laptop is already closed. You write one short note for the next interview, then put the notebook away.

Nothing outside you has changed yet.

But the boundary around your responsibility is clearer, and waiting no longer has to pretend it is work.

2. Choose a next action that can finish without another person’s permission

Now imagine the waiting is about a relationship rather than a job.

There was a tense exchange in a WhatsApp group with a friend or coworker, and you later sent an apology. The message has been read, but no reply has come.

You may want the other person to forgive you.

That hope is real, but “make them forgive me” is not an action you can complete.

You can make sure the apology says what you are sorry for. You can correct something practical if it can still be corrected. You can also avoid sending another message only to make your own waiting feel easier.

After that, the other person may need time.

The same move works with a proposal or request. Suppose you have sent a price proposal for a small design job to a local food business in Surabaya.

Result I hope for:
The client accepts my proposal.

Part that needs their decision:
Whether they say yes.

Part I can finish:
Check what I promised, send any missing detail, and choose when I will look for a reply again.

The last line gives waiting a next check point. That means you choose a later moment to look again because a new reply could then change what you do.

Until then, reopening email or WhatsApp every few minutes does not add progress.

You might put the phone in your bag while you eat, ride home, or begin another piece of work.

You are not acting as if the answer is unimportant. You are giving the waiting a boundary.

3. When the answer comes, separate what happened from how well you acted

A few days later, the reply finally arrives.

Perhaps the recruiter chose someone else. Perhaps the small business says no to the proposal. Perhaps the person you apologised to still wants distance.

Now two questions are useful:

What happened?

And:

What does this result actually tell me about the part that was mine?

Those questions can have different answers.

A proposal can be careful and clear even if the client rejects it. A proposal can also be accepted even though part of it was rushed.

So the result matters, but it is not a perfect score for the quality of your action.

Use only the information the result really gives you. If several interviews reveal the same missing skill, that pattern deserves attention. If several clients misunderstand the same part of your proposal, that wording may need to change.

Imagine reading a rejection message while sitting in a café before heading home.

The disappointment may arrive first.

Then you return to the control line: did this message give you new information that changes something you can do?

If yes, you have a new action.

If no, there may be nothing more to solve in this situation today.

The answer can still hurt. It simply does not need to create extra work that is not yours.

First separate the roles, then finish your part, then learn from the answer

These three parts belong together because each solves a different problem.

The control line shows where your responsibility stops. A finishable next action keeps the waiting period from spreading across the whole day. The final result can then give you information for a later attempt.

If you skip the first part, you may keep sending messages after your work is already complete. If you skip the second, you may understand the boundary but still spend the evening circling it. If you ignore the result, you may miss feedback that could genuinely change what you do next.

The goal is not to stop caring.

It is to care about the answer while leaving the other person’s decision with the person who has to make it.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s take one answer you are waiting for and divide the situation into Still mine and Not mine.
  • Let’s choose one useful action from the first side that can honestly be finished today.
  • Let’s decide when we will check for new information again, then give the time in between back to the rest of our day.
