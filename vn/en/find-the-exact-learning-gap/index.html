When You Know a Lesson but Cannot Say What Is Missing: Find the Learning Gap

Use three simple checks to tell whether you are missing a meaning, a connection, or the point where a rule stops working.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 8/31/2026 Updated 8/31/2026 Information checked 8/30/2026 7 min read

This article is for you if

You can follow a lesson while it is open, but later you cannot tell which part you truly understand and which part is still unclear.

By the end, you will be able to

You will be able to separate a missing meaning, a missing connection, and a missing limit, then choose a smaller question instead of restarting the whole topic.

The lesson felt fine five minutes ago. Can we find the exact point where your explanation now breaks?

Maybe you watched part of a lesson after school, during a break at university, or at home after work.

While the screen was open, the steps seemed to fit. When you put the phone down or close the laptop and try to explain the idea yourself, one part is suddenly hard to say.

Sometimes one important word is still unclear. Sometimes the words are clear but the reason between two steps is missing. Sometimes the rule works in the example, but you do not know the condition that would make it stop working.

Those problems can all feel like one fog.

They need different questions.

Let’s find the gap before we search for another full lesson

  • Let’s see whether one important term still has no clear meaning.
  • Let’s see whether we can explain why one step leads to the next.
  • Let’s see whether we know the condition where the rule stops working in the same way.

1. Check whether one important word is familiar but still unclear

A lesson can feel much harder when one key word has never become a clear idea in your head.

You may recognise the term from a school slide, a university lesson, or a short training video. But if someone asks what it means, your answer may repeat the same term without explaining it.

That is a meaning gap: the label is familiar, but the idea behind it is still blurry.

Psychologists Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil studied a related problem called the illusion of explanatory depth. In simple terms, people can feel that they understand how something works in more detail than they can actually explain. Their 2002 paper tested explanations of familiar systems and devices, not online courses, so it does not show that every learning problem comes from this effect. It does show why trying to explain an idea can reveal limits that familiarity hides. (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002)

A small check can help:

Can I explain the important word without using the same word again?

Imagine you are reviewing percentage change for a maths lesson on your phone before joining a study group.

You can follow the formula on the screen, but “percentage change” still means little more than “the number the formula gives me.”

Try a plain sentence instead:

Percentage change tells me how large a change is compared with where I started.

Now the term has something you can picture: a starting point, a movement, and a comparison between them.

The whole topic may still need work. But one hidden problem is now visible, so you no longer have to treat every part of the lesson as equally unclear.

2. Check whether you know both steps but cannot explain why they connect

A different gap appears when the important words already make sense, but the logic between two steps does not.

You understand the first step. You understand the second. What you cannot explain is why the first one leads to the second.

That is a connection gap.

Research on self-explanation gives us a useful reason to test this kind of gap. Self-explanation simply means trying to explain the material to yourself while you learn it. In a 1994 study, Michelene Chi and colleagues asked 14 eighth-grade students to explain each line of a text about the circulatory system as they read. Ten students in a comparison group read the same text twice without those prompts. The students who were asked to explain showed a larger gain from the researchers’ pretest to posttest. It was a small study in one subject, so it does not prove that self-explanation always improves learning. It supports the narrower point that producing an explanation can help reveal and build links that reading alone may leave weak. (Chi et al., 1994)

Suppose you are checking a spreadsheet for a class project with friends in Hanoi, Da Nang, or any other city. A value rises from 80 to 100.

You know the amount of change is 20. You also remember that the usual percentage-change calculation divides 20 by 80.

Then ask:

Why 80?

If the only answer you have is “because that is the formula,” you have found the missing bridge.

The reason is:

We are comparing the size of the change with the value we started from.

So 20 divided by 80 is 0.25. The increase is 25%.

Now the calculation answers a clear question: the rise of 20 is one quarter of the starting value.

Instead of opening another complete tutorial, you now have a smaller question to work on:

Why does this step follow from the one before it?

3. Check whether you know the rule but not the condition where it fails

There is another kind of uncertainty that can hide behind a correct answer.

You know the important words, and you can explain why the steps connect. But all the examples you have seen may use conditions where the rule works normally.

What may still be missing is its boundary. Here, a boundary means the condition where a rule no longer works in the same way.

Use the same percentage-change idea, but imagine you are testing a small spreadsheet before using it for a club budget or a family household record.

Suppose a value moves from 0 to 20.

The amount of change is still easy to find. It is 20.

But the usual percentage-change calculation compares that change with the starting value. That would require dividing by 0, and division by zero is undefined.

So the usual formula cannot give an ordinary percentage change when the starting value is zero.

The earlier explanation was not wrong. You have reached a condition where the rule needs different treatment.

That gives you a third question:

What could I change in this example that would make the rule stop working in the same way?

You do not need to collect every possible exception.

One clear edge can show whether you understand the rule itself or only remember a pattern that happened to work in the first example.

Three checks can turn a vague feeling into one workable question

These checks are most useful in this order because each one removes a different kind of uncertainty.

Start with meaning. If an important word is still empty, later reasoning has nothing firm to build on.

Then check the connection. If the words are clear but the bridge between two steps is missing, another definition may not solve the problem.

Finally, check the limit. If the meaning and the connection are both clear, changing one condition can show whether you understand the rule beyond the example you were given.

Think of this as a practical way to inspect confusion, not as a scientific classification of every learning problem. More than one gap can also be present at once.

The useful change is that “I don’t understand” no longer has to describe the whole lesson.

“Which meaning is unclear?”

“Which connection is missing?”

“Where does this rule stop working in the same way?”

Each question gives you somewhere specific to begin.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s choose one confusing idea and explain its most important word in plain language.
  • Let’s put two steps beside each other and ask what makes the first lead to the second.
  • Let’s change one condition in a small example and see whether the rule still works in the same way.

You do not need to prove that you understand the whole subject today.

We are only trying to locate the next missing piece clearly enough to work on it.

Once the gap has a name, the next question becomes easier to ask.
