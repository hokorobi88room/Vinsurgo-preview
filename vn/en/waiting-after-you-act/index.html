After You Have Done Your Part: 3 Ways to Stop Waiting Like There Is More to Fix

Use a simple distinction from Epictetus to see what still needs action from you, what now belongs to someone else, and what the final answer can teach you.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 9/1/2026 Updated 9/1/2026 Information checked 8/29/2026 6 min read

This article is for you if

You have already acted, but you keep checking or replaying the situation because the final answer now depends partly on someone else.

By the end, you will be able to

You will be able to separate what is still yours to do from what is not, finish one useful next action, and use the eventual result as information rather than a verdict on yourself.

We already pressed send. Why does it still feel as if we are supposed to do something?

You finish a video interview after work in Ho Chi Minh City and close the laptop.

A few minutes later, you check your phone.

No reply.

You start another task, hear the phone move on the table, and look again.

Still nothing.

When the answer matters, silence can make the situation feel active even when there is nothing useful to do yet.

Your mind may start offering extra work: send another message, rewrite what you already sent, guess what the other person is thinking.

But an unfinished situation is not always unfinished work on your side.

In Elizabeth Carter’s English translation of Epictetus’s Enchiridion, section 1 begins:

“Some things are in our control and others not.”

The same passage places “whatever are our own actions” among the things in our control, while reputation and command are listed among things that are not. You can read the passage in the MIT Internet Classics Archive.

For an ordinary day, the distinction becomes one useful question:

What still needs an action from me, and what now has to happen outside me?

Let’s stop giving silence extra jobs

  • Let’s mark where our part ends and another person’s choice begins.
  • Let’s choose one action we can finish without waiting for permission.
  • Let’s use the eventual answer to learn, not to decide whether our whole effort was good or bad.

1. Separate the part you can still complete from the part you cannot decide

Imagine you have completed an online interview from your room in Ho Chi Minh City.

You hope to be selected, but the thought “I need them to choose me” joins your work and their decision into one problem.

Your side includes how you prepared, how clearly you answered, whether you promised to send anything later, and what you can improve.

Their side includes whom else they speak with, what the role needs, when they decide, and whom they choose.

Epictetus’s distinction helps us keep those sides apart. It does not mean that the outcome is unimportant; it means that another person’s action cannot be performed by you.

Try making a simple control line:

Still mine

  • Send the information I promised.
  • Note the interview question I found difficult.
  • Prepare a clearer answer before the next suitable interview.

Not mine

  • The final choice.
  • Who else is being considered.
  • The exact time the reply arrives.
  • The opinion someone forms after the conversation.

The second side can matter a great deal without becoming yours to control.

Picture the evening traffic outside while your laptop is already shut. You write one useful note for the next interview, put the pen down, and let the company make the decision that belongs to it.

Nothing has been decided yet.

But you have stopped confusing waiting with work.

2. Give yourself a next action with a real stopping point

Suppose the waiting comes after a difficult message to someone close to you in Hanoi.

You have apologised, but the other person has not answered.

You may want forgiveness, but “make them forgive me” is not something you can finish on your own.

You can check whether your apology clearly names what you did. You can correct something practical if it can still be corrected, then leave room for the other person to decide what they need.

After that, your immediate action may be complete even if the relationship is still unsettled.

The same method works after sending work to someone. Imagine you have sent a proposal to a small client in Da Nang and are waiting to hear back.

Result I hope for:
The client accepts my proposal.

What depends on them:
Whether they say yes.

What I can finish:
Check what I promised, send anything that is genuinely missing, and choose when I will look for a reply again.

That last step creates a next check point. It is simply a later time when new information could give you a real decision to make.

Until then, reopening email or a chat app every few minutes does not add a new action.

You can put the phone away while you eat, ride home, or begin another piece of work.

The answer still matters.

It just does not need to occupy every minute before it exists.

3. When the answer arrives, treat it as information rather than a verdict

A few days later, the reply reaches you while you are sitting in a café before heading home.

It says no.

Now it helps to separate two questions:

What happened?

And:

What does this result actually tell me about the part that was mine?

A clear, careful proposal can still be rejected. A rushed proposal can sometimes be accepted.

So the outcome matters, but it is not a perfect score for the quality of your action.

Use only the information the result can honestly give you.

If several people misunderstand the same part of your proposal, the wording may need work. If several interviews reveal the same missing skill, that repeated pattern may point to something worth practising.

There may still be disappointment when you read the answer.

Return to the control line and ask whether the new information changes something you can do.

If it does, you have a new action.

If it does not, there may be nothing more to solve in this situation today.

The result can hurt without becoming a command to keep working on what is no longer yours.

First mark the boundary, then finish your part, then learn from what comes back

The three steps belong together because they solve different problems.

The control line keeps another person’s choice from becoming part of your workload. A finishable next action gives your effort somewhere useful to go. The final result then gives you information that may improve a later attempt.

The order matters.

If you skip the boundary, you may keep sending messages after your useful work is finished. If you skip the next action, you may understand the situation but still spend the evening circling it.

If you ignore the result, you may miss feedback that really does deserve a change.

The goal is not to stop caring.

It is to care about the answer without trying to perform a decision that belongs partly to someone else.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s take one answer we are waiting for and divide the situation into Still mine and Not mine.
  • Let’s choose one useful action from the first side that can honestly reach an ending today.
  • Let’s decide when we will check for new information again, then return the time in between to the rest of our day.
