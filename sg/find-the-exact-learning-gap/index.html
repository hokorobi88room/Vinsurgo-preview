If a Lesson Makes Sense Until the Screen Closes, Find the Gap Before You Relearn It

Use three questions to separate an unclear meaning, a broken link in the reasoning, and the boundary of a rule.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 31/08/2026 Updated 31/08/2026 Information checked 30/08/2026 7 min read

This article is for you if

You can follow a lesson while the explanation is visible, but later you cannot tell exactly which part of your understanding has disappeared.

By the end, you will be able to

You will be able to separate a missing meaning, a missing connection, and a missing limit, then choose a smaller question instead of restarting the whole topic.

The video is closed, your notes are still there, and now one part will not come out in words. Can we locate it together?

Maybe you were reviewing a lesson on the MRT, looking through notes between classes, or finishing a short course after work.

While the explanation was open, the steps felt orderly. A few minutes later, you can remember the page but not the exact part that made the idea make sense.

One possibility is that a key word never became clear. Another is that you understand two steps but not the reason that joins them. A third is that you know the rule only in the kind of example you were shown.

All three can produce the same sentence: “I don’t get it.”

But each one asks for a different repair.

Let’s narrow the problem before we collect more explanations

  • Let’s ask whether the key term means something clear in our own words.
  • Let’s ask what makes one step lead to the next.
  • Let’s ask what change in the conditions would make the rule stop working normally.

1. Test whether the key word has a real meaning in your own words

Sometimes the difficulty is smaller than the whole lesson. One important term may still be only a familiar label.

You might have seen it on a school slide, in a polytechnic module, in a university lecture, or in workplace training. Recognition is easy; explaining the idea without repeating the term is harder.

That is what we will call a meaning gap: you know the label, but the idea behind it is not yet clear enough to use.

Rozenblit and Keil studied a related mismatch and called it the illusion of explanatory depth. Their 2002 research looked at people explaining familiar systems and devices, not online learning, so it cannot tell us that every unclear lesson has the same cause. The useful caution is narrower: something can feel well understood while our own explanation still contains holes. (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002)

Try this check:

Can I explain the term without using the term itself?

Suppose you are revising percentage change on your phone while waiting to change MRT lines.

You can follow the formula, yet “percentage change” still means little more than “the final percentage I calculate.”

Give it a plain-language meaning instead:

Percentage change compares the size of a change with the value you started from.

Now there is a simple picture behind the words: a starting point, a movement away from it, and a comparison between the two.

You may still have more to learn, but you have already stopped blaming the entire topic. You have found one term that needs a clearer meaning.

2. Test whether the missing part is the reason between two steps

Sometimes every important word is clear, yet the reasoning still breaks in the middle.

You can explain the step before the gap and the step after it. What is missing is the sentence that tells you why one leads to the other.

That is a connection gap.

A 1994 study by Michelene Chi and colleagues gives a related reason to pay attention to this kind of break. Fourteen eighth-grade students were prompted to explain each line of a text about the circulatory system to themselves, while ten comparison students read the same text twice without those prompts. The prompted group showed a larger gain from pretest to posttest on the researchers’ measures of understanding. Because the study was small and focused on one subject, it does not justify saying that self-explanation always works. What it does support is the narrower idea that producing an explanation can help reveal and build links that reading alone may leave weak. (Chi et al., 1994)

Imagine you are checking a spreadsheet for a group assignment in a campus study room. One value has moved from 80 to 100.

You know the change is 20. You also remember that the usual percentage-change calculation uses 20 divided by 80.

Now pause at the part people often skip:

Why is 80 the number underneath?

If the answer in your head is only “that is what the formula says,” the missing piece is no longer vague.

The bridge is this:

The change is being measured against the starting value.

Twenty divided by 80 is 0.25, so the increase is 25%.

That result now has a reason behind it: the rise of 20 is one quarter of the value you began with.

Instead of searching for another complete lesson on percentages, you can ask one much sharper question:

What makes this step follow from the step before it?

3. Test whether you know the rule only inside its comfortable examples

A correct answer can still hide a gap.

You may know the term and the reasoning, but only for examples where every condition lets the rule work normally.

The missing piece may be the rule’s boundary. Here, a boundary means a condition where the same rule cannot be used in the same way.

Keep the percentage-change example, but imagine you are testing a simple sheet before using it for a CCA event budget or a small team record.

This time, the value moves from 0 to 20.

The amount of change is still 20.

The usual percentage-change calculation would compare that change with the starting value. That requires division by 0, and division by zero is undefined.

So the ordinary percentage-change formula cannot produce a normal percentage change when the starting value is zero.

The earlier explanation has not collapsed. You have simply reached a condition where the rule needs different treatment.

That gives you the third check:

What could I change in this example that would stop the rule from working in the usual way?

You do not need a catalogue of every exception. One clear edge is enough to test whether you understand the rule itself rather than only the pattern of the first example.

Meaning first, connection second, boundary third

These checks are not three versions of the same advice. Each one asks about a different part of understanding.

Start with meaning because reasoning built on an unclear term will stay unstable. Then look for the missing connection, because another definition will not repair a bridge between two ideas.

Only after those two are clear does it make sense to test the boundary. Changing one condition then shows whether you understand the rule beyond the example that introduced it.

This is a practical diagnostic, not a complete scientific classification of every learning problem. You can also have more than one kind of gap at the same time.

The important shift is from one large sentence to three smaller questions:

“Which term is still unclear?”

“What reason connects these two steps?”

“What condition makes this rule stop working normally?”

Each question points to a different next move.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s take one confusing topic and put its most important term into plain words of our own.
  • Let’s place two steps side by side and write the reason that links them.
  • Let’s change one condition in a tiny example and see whether the rule still survives.

You do not need to finish the whole subject in one sitting.

We are trying to name the next missing piece accurately enough that you know what to ask, test, or review next.

Once the gap becomes specific, learning can become specific too.
