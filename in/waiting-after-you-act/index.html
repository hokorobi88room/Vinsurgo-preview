Waiting for Someone Else's Answer: 3 Ways to Stay With What Is Yours

After an interview, message, apology or application, separate your part from the decision you cannot make for someone else.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 1/9/2026 Updated 1/9/2026 Information checked 29/8/2026 7 min read

This article is for you if

You have already taken the step you could take, but you keep checking your phone or replaying the situation because someone else's answer still matters.

By the end, you will be able to

You will be able to mark what is still yours to do, finish one useful action, and learn from the eventual result without making it a verdict on yourself.

We have sent the message. Why does the silence still feel like work?

You finish a campus-placement interview or a video interview after work, close the laptop, and tell yourself that the difficult part is over.

Then the phone lights up.

Not the message you are waiting for.

You put it down, start doing something else, and check again a little later.

The answer matters, so your mind keeps searching for another action: Was that answer too weak? Should I send a follow-up now? Does no reply already mean no?

There is a useful distinction here. A situation can still be unfinished even when your part in it is finished for now.

In Elizabeth Carter’s English translation of a short work by the ancient philosopher Epictetus, the Enchiridion, section 1 begins:

“Some things are in our control and others not.”

The same passage includes “whatever are our own actions” among the things in our control, while it places things such as reputation and command outside that group. You can read the passage in the MIT Internet Classics Archive.

For an ordinary day, we can turn the old distinction into one question:

What part of this situation still needs an action from me?

Let’s make the waiting period clearer

  • Let’s separate our part from the decision another person has to make.
  • Let’s pick one action with a real stopping point instead of keeping the situation open all day.
  • Let’s read the eventual result for what it can teach us, without using it as a score for our whole worth.

1. Mark where your part stops and the other person’s part begins

Imagine you have just finished a campus recruitment interview.

You naturally want to be selected, but “I need them to choose me” contains more than one person’s work.

Your side includes how you prepared, how clearly you answered, whether you send anything you promised, and what you decide to improve afterwards.

Their side includes whom else they meet, what the role needs, when the team decides, and whom they finally select.

Epictetus’s distinction is useful because it stops those two sides from becoming one blurred problem. His point is not that outside results are meaningless; it is that another person’s action is not something you can perform for them.

Try drawing a simple control line after an interview:

Still mine

  • Send the document or detail I said I would send.
  • Note the question that exposed a weak spot in my answer.
  • Prepare that answer more clearly for the next suitable interview.

Not mine

  • The recruiter’s final choice.
  • Who else is in the process.
  • The exact hour when the reply comes.
  • The opinion another person forms after the conversation ends.

The second side can still matter to you. It simply does not become more controllable because you stare at it for longer.

Picture the laptop closing while the ceiling fan keeps turning above you. You write one useful note for the next interview, then leave the result where it actually belongs.

Nothing outside you has changed yet, but the boundary around your responsibility has become much cleaner.

2. Choose a next action that can actually finish

Now suppose the waiting is not about work.

You sent an apology after a tense exchange in a family chat, and the message has been read but nobody has answered.

You may want the other person to forgive you. That hope is real, but “make them forgive me” cannot be completed by you alone.

What can be completed is smaller and clearer. You can make sure the apology names what you did, avoid adding another message just to reduce your own discomfort, and correct something practical if it is still possible to correct.

After that, the other person may need time.

This same move works with a client proposal, an application or a request:

Result I hope for:
The client accepts my proposal.

Part that needs their decision:
Whether they say yes.

Part I can finish:
Check the proposal once, send what I promised, and decide when I will check for a reply again.

That last line creates a next check point. It is simply a time when new information could give you a real new decision to make.

Until then, reopening email or a chat app every few minutes does not create extra progress.

You might put the phone away while you have dinner, travel home, or begin the next piece of work. You are not acting as if the reply is unimportant; you are giving waiting a boundary.

3. When the answer comes, separate the result from the quality of your action

A few days later, the answer may finally arrive.

Perhaps a college application does not work out, a recruiter chooses another candidate, or a client decides not to continue.

At that point, it helps to ask two separate questions: What happened? and What does this result tell me about the part that was mine?

Those questions do not always produce the same judgment.

A proposal can be careful and clear even if the client rejects it. A proposal can also be accepted even though you rushed parts of it.

So the result matters, but it is not a perfect measurement of how well you acted. It may also tell you very little about why the result happened.

Use only the information the result actually gives you. If several interviews reveal the same missing skill, that pattern deserves attention; if several people misunderstand the same sentence in your proposal, the wording may need to change.

Imagine opening the message after days of waiting and seeing a no.

The disappointment may be immediate, but the control line gives you somewhere sensible to return. Does this answer reveal a useful next action, or is there simply nothing more to solve in this situation today?

If there is something to learn, take it forward. If there is not, you do not need to turn the disappointment into extra work just to feel busy.

First draw the boundary, then act, then learn from what comes back

The three parts belong in this order because each solves a different problem.

The control line shows where your responsibility ends. A finishable next action prevents waiting from taking over the rest of the day. The final result may then give you information that can improve a later attempt.

Skip the first step and you may keep sending messages after your work is already done. Skip the second and you may understand the boundary but still spend the evening circling the same thought; ignore the third and you may miss feedback that genuinely deserves a change.

The goal is not to stop caring about the answer. It is to care about it without claiming responsibility for a decision that belongs partly to somebody else.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s take one reply you are waiting for and place each part on either side of a Still mine / Not mine line.
  • Let’s choose one useful step from the first side that can honestly be finished today.
  • Let’s decide when we will check for new information again, then return the time in between to the rest of our day.
