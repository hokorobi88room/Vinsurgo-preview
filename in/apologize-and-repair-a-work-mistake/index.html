Made a Mistake at Work? How to Apologise and Put It Right

A useful apology names the mistake, reduces the extra work it caused, and shows the check you will add next.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 30/8/2026 Updated 30/8/2026 Information checked 29/8/2026 7 min read

This article is for you if

You made a work mistake that caused confusion, delay or extra effort for someone else, and you want to apologise without sounding defensive.

By the end, you will be able to

You will have a clear three-part way to acknowledge the mistake, repair what you can, and explain the next check without assuming that one apology will immediately rebuild trust.

You know you need to apologise, but your mind is already preparing excuses. Can we make the conversation about repair instead?

Perhaps you sent last week’s spreadsheet just before a morning review.

Perhaps you missed a handover, and a teammate in another city had to wait before they could continue their part.

The moment you notice the mistake, your mind can race towards reasons. You were replying from your phone during the commute, two files looked almost the same, or another request arrived at the same time.

Those details may be true. They still do not answer the first thing the other person needs to know: do you understand what happened, and what will you do now?

Let’s make the apology easier to trust.

  • Let’s say what we did and what it affected before explaining the background.
  • Let’s offer a repair that takes some of the extra work back onto us.
  • Let’s name one check we will add next, then leave space for the other person to respond.

1. Say what happened before you defend yourself

Embarrassment can make an explanation come out before responsibility.

“I’m sorry, but the morning was hectic.”

“I thought this was the latest sheet.”

“I didn’t realise you needed it before the call.”

Any of those details could be true. If they come first, however, the other person may still be listening for one basic answer: are you accepting your part in the mistake?

Imagine you shared an older spreadsheet in the team chat before a morning review, and a colleague checked the wrong figures.

A clearer opening would be:

“I sent the older sheet, so you reviewed the wrong version. I’m sorry. I should have checked the file before I shared it.”

That sentence does three useful things. It names your action, names the effect, and makes your responsibility visible.

A 2016 study by Roy Lewicki, Beth Polin and Robert Lount examined six parts of apologies across two studies using controlled trust-violation situations. The parts included regret, explanation, taking responsibility, an offer to repair the harm, a statement about change, and a request for forgiveness; apologies containing more of those parts were generally judged more effective than apologies containing fewer. (Original study)

The study does not give us one perfect script for every workplace or culture. It does support a useful distinction: saying “I’m sorry” and clearly stating what you did are separate parts of an apology.

Sometimes you know your mistake but do not yet know its full effect.

Then you can say:

“I sent the wrong version. I’m sorry. I know that may have interrupted your work. What did it affect on your side?”

You have already owned the mistake. Now you are checking the real impact instead of guessing.

2. Offer a repair that removes some of the extra work

A sincere apology can still leave the other person carrying the practical cost.

Suppose a teammate has spent part of the afternoon adding comments to an old presentation because you sent the wrong deck before a client call.

“I’m really sorry. I’ll send the correct file now.”

That replaces the file. It may not replace the time they already spent.

A stronger repair could sound like this:

“I’ll send the correct deck now. If you have already added comments to the old one, I can move them across so you do not have to repeat the work.”

Now the repair has a shape the other person can actually use.

The same 2016 study treated an offer of repair as one of the six apology components it tested. That does not mean every problem can be undone, or that offering a fix automatically restores trust. (Original study)

In ordinary work, the repair might be replacing a file, correcting a figure, restoring missing information, or contacting a person who received the wrong message.

If you are not sure what would help most, you can still bring a possible solution:

“I can correct this part now, and I can also redo the handover if that saves you time. Is there another effect I have missed?”

That is different from saying only, “What do you want me to do?”

You are not asking the person affected by your mistake to design the whole repair for you.

Some harm cannot be fully repaired. A serious mistake involving safety, money, formal duties or another person’s rights may require reporting, correction or qualified professional help in addition to a personal apology.

The aim is not to make the problem disappear through careful wording. It is to make your next action fit the part that can actually be repaired.

3. Show one new check instead of promising perfection

After a mistake, “It will never happen again” can feel like the strongest promise available.

The problem is that you cannot fully control the future.

A better ending points to the exact weak spot you found.

Imagine two similar files are sitting in a shared folder, and you picked the wrong one before sending it to the team from home.

Instead of promising that you will never repeat the mistake, you could say:

“I mixed up two files with similar names. Before I mark a handover as complete, I’ll open the attachment from the sent message and check that it is the right version.”

Now the promised change has a concrete form.

You are not trying to prove that you are a careful person. You are showing one check designed for the failure that actually happened.

Then leave room for the other person’s response.

They may accept the apology straight away. They may still be irritated, need time, or tell you about an effect you did not see.

You cannot control that part.

A simple ending could be:

“I’m sorry for the extra work. I’ve corrected the file and changed the check I use before sending. If I missed another effect, tell me and I’ll look at it.”

After that, let the conversation breathe unless another fact needs to be cleared up.

Your apology is your decision; forgiveness belongs to the other person. You can own your side without asking them to reassure you straight away.

For a small mistake, that may be enough. If trust was damaged more deeply, the apology may only be the beginning, and what you do afterwards will matter too.

A useful apology moves through three different moments

First, name the mistake so both of you have the same picture of what happened. This keeps the past clear before explanations start to blur it.

Next, repair what you reasonably can in the present. Regret becomes more useful when it removes some of the extra work, delay or confusion you caused.

Finally, show the check you will add for the future. This is more credible than promising perfection because it connects the change to the exact weak point you found.

The order matters. If you jump straight to prevention, you can skip over the harm; if you stay only with regret, the other person may still carry the practical cost; and if you offer a fix without owning the mistake, the repair can feel separate from the apology.

A clear apology cannot force trust to return. It can make your responsibility, your repair and your next action easier to see.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s describe the mistake in one sentence without adding “but.”
  • Let’s name one repair we can offer before asking the other person to solve the whole problem.
  • Let’s choose one check tied to the actual failure instead of promising that we will never make a mistake again.
