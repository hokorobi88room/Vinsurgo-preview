When a Lesson Feels Clear but You Still Cannot Explain It: Find the Exact Learning Gap

Check whether you are missing a meaning, a connection, or the limit of a rule before you restart the whole topic.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 31/8/2026 Updated 31/8/2026 Information checked 30/8/2026 7 min read

This article is for you if

You can follow a class, video, or work tutorial while it is open, but later you are not sure which part you truly understood.

By the end, you will be able to

You will be able to separate a missing meaning, a missing connection, and a missing limit, then choose a smaller question instead of restarting the whole topic.

The lesson made sense on the screen. So why does it disappear when you try to explain it?

Maybe you watched the lesson on your phone during a bus or metro ride, or opened it again after dinner at home.

While the teacher or video was guiding you, each step seemed to follow the last one. Then you close the screen and try to say the idea yourself.

A word feels empty. A step has no reason. Or the rule works in the example, but you do not know when it would fail.

From inside your head, all three can feel like the same sentence: “I don’t understand.”

They are different gaps, and we can check them one by one.

Let’s make the confusion smaller

  • Let’s check whether one important word still has no clear meaning.
  • Let’s check whether we understand why one step leads to the next.
  • Let’s check whether we know the condition where the rule stops working in the same way.

1. Check whether one important word is still only a label

Sometimes one unclear term makes an entire lesson feel harder than it is.

You may recognise the word each time it appears in a coaching note, college slide, or office training video. But if you cannot explain it without repeating the same word, the meaning may still be weak.

That is a meaning gap: the label is familiar, but the idea behind it is still blurry.

Psychologists Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil studied a related effect called the illusion of explanatory depth. In simple terms, people can feel that they understand how something works in more detail than they can actually explain. Their 2002 paper studied explanations of familiar systems and devices, not online courses, so it does not show that every learning problem comes from this effect. It does show why trying to explain an idea can expose limits that familiarity can hide. (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002)

You can use one small check:

Can I explain the important word without using the same word again?

Imagine you are learning percentage change for a class, an entrance test, or a spreadsheet task at work.

You can follow the formula, but “percentage change” still means little more than “the answer the formula gives me.”

Try a plain sentence instead:

Percentage change tells me how large a change is compared with where I started.

Now the term has a picture behind it. There is a starting point, there is movement, and you are comparing the movement with that starting point.

Your notes may still need more work, but the first gap is no longer invisible.

2. Check whether you know the pieces but not the bridge between them

A different problem appears when the important words make sense, but the logic between two steps disappears.

You understand the first idea. You understand the second idea. What you cannot explain is why the first one leads to the second.

That is a connection gap.

Research on self-explanation gives us a useful reason to test this gap. Self-explanation means trying to explain the material to yourself while you learn it. In a 1994 study, Michelene Chi and colleagues asked 14 eighth-grade students to explain each line of a text about the circulatory system as they read. Ten students in a comparison group read the same text twice without those prompts. The prompted group showed a larger gain from pretest to posttest. It was a small study in one subject, so it does not prove that self-explanation always improves learning. It supports the narrower idea that producing an explanation can help reveal and build links that reading alone may leave weak. (Chi et al., 1994)

Suppose you are checking a spreadsheet for a class project or an office task. A value rises from 80 to 100.

You know the amount of change is 20. You also know that the usual percentage-change calculation divides 20 by 80.

Then ask:

Why 80?

If your answer is only “because that is the formula,” the missing part is not the definition of percentage change. It is the bridge between the amount of change and the starting value.

The bridge is:

We are comparing the size of the change with the value we started from.

So 20 divided by 80 is 0.25. The increase is 25%.

Now the calculation answers a clear question. A rise of 20 is one quarter of the starting value.

Instead of searching for another full lesson on percentages, you can ask the smaller question:

Why does this step follow from the one before it?

3. Check whether you know the rule but not its edge

A correct answer can still hide one more gap.

You know the words, and you can explain why the steps connect. But perhaps you have only seen cases where the rule works normally.

You do not yet know its boundary. A boundary is simply the point where a rule no longer works in the same way.

Use the same percentage-change idea. This time, imagine you are testing a spreadsheet formula before you depend on it for a report, assignment, or practice question.

Suppose a value moves from 0 to 20.

The amount of change is 20. But the usual percentage-change calculation compares that change with the starting value, which would require dividing by 0.

Division by zero is undefined, so the usual formula cannot give an ordinary percentage change when the starting value is zero.

Nothing was wrong with the earlier explanation. You have found one condition where the rule needs different treatment.

That gives us a third question:

What could I change in this example that would make the rule stop working in the same way?

You do not need to search for every possible exception.

One clear edge is enough to show that you understand more than a pattern you can repeat.

One sentence of confusion can hide three different gaps

These checks work best in this order because each one deals with a different problem.

First, check the meaning. If an important word is still empty, later reasoning has nothing solid to build on.

Second, check the connection. If the words are clear but the bridge between two steps is missing, another definition may not help. You need to explain why one idea leads to the next.

Third, check the limit. If the meaning and the connection are both clear, changing one condition can show whether you understand the rule itself or only the example you were shown.

This is a practical three-question check, not a scientific list of every possible learning problem. You may sometimes have more than one gap at once.

The point is to stop treating all confusion as one giant problem.

“I don’t understand” is hard to act on.

“Which word is unclear?”

“Which connection is missing?”

“Where does this rule stop working?”

Those are questions you can work with.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s choose one confusing idea from a class, tutorial, or work task and explain its most important word in plain language.
  • Let’s find two steps we understand separately and ask what connects them.
  • Let’s change one condition in a small example and see whether the rule still works in the same way.

You do not need to prove that you understand everything today.

We are only trying to find the next missing piece clearly enough to work on it.

A smaller question can make the next step much easier to see.
