When a Lesson Feels Familiar but You Still Cannot Explain It: Name the Exact Gap

Use three checks—meaning, connection, and boundary—to turn ‘I don’t understand’ into one smaller problem you can actually work on.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 31/08/2026 Updated 31/08/2026 Information checked 30/08/2026 7 min read

This article is for you if

You can follow a lesson or tutorial while it is open, but later you cannot tell exactly which part you understood and which part is still missing.

By the end, you will be able to

You will be able to separate a meaning gap, a connection gap, and a boundary gap, then choose a smaller question instead of restarting the whole topic.

The lesson made sense while it was open. Can we find the exact point that disappeared when you tried to explain it?

Imagine finishing a short lesson on your phone after class or after work. A class group chat is still active, your notes are beside you, and the explanation feels familiar.

Then you try to say the idea in your own words.

One part refuses to come out.

Maybe one important word still has no clear meaning. Maybe you know two steps but cannot explain why one leads to the other. Or maybe the rule works in the example, but you do not know when it stops working.

Those three problems can all sound like “I don’t understand.”

They need different fixes.

Let’s make the gap small enough to see

  • Let’s check whether one key word still has no clear meaning in our own words.
  • Let’s check whether the missing part is the reason joining two steps.
  • Let’s check whether we know where the rule stops working in the usual way.

1. Check whether one key word is familiar but still unclear

Sometimes one term makes an entire lesson feel harder than it really is.

You may recognise the word from a slide, a note, or a tutorial. You may even know where it appeared. But if someone asks what it means, your explanation may only repeat the same word.

Call this a meaning gap: you know the label when you see it, but the idea underneath has not become clear yet.

Psychologists Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil studied a related effect called the illusion of explanatory depth. In ordinary language, people can feel that they understand how something works in more detail than they can actually explain. Their 2002 paper examined explanations of familiar systems and devices, not online lessons, so it does not show that every learning problem comes from this effect. It does show why trying to explain something can expose limits that simple familiarity can hide. (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002)

Try one small test:

Can I explain this important word without using the same word again?

Suppose you are revising percentage change on your phone before the next class, while messages from your class group keep arriving.

You can follow the formula, but “percentage change” still means little more than “the percentage I get after the calculation.”

Put it into ordinary words instead:

Percentage change compares the size of a change with the value at the starting point.

Now the term has a simple picture behind it: a starting point, a movement, and a comparison between the two.

The whole topic may still need work. But you have found one precise thing that needs a clearer meaning.

2. Check whether you know both steps but not the reason between them

A second kind of confusion appears when the words make sense but the reasoning breaks in the middle.

You understand the first step. You understand the next step. What is missing is the reason that connects them.

That is a connection gap.

A 1994 study by Michelene Chi and colleagues gives a useful reason to test this kind of gap. 14 eighth-grade students were prompted to explain each line of a text about the circulatory system to themselves, while ten students in a comparison group read the same text twice without those prompts. The prompted group showed a larger gain from pretest to posttest on the researchers’ measures of understanding. The study was small and focused on one subject, so it does not prove that self-explanation always improves learning. It supports the narrower point that producing an explanation can help reveal and build links that reading alone may leave weak. (Chi et al., 1994)

Imagine you are helping to prepare simple figures for a student association or a small community event. A spreadsheet shows a count rising from 80 to 100.

You know the increase is 20. You also remember that the usual percentage-change calculation divides 20 by 80.

Now stop at the step that is easy to copy:

Why 80?

If your only answer is “because that is the formula,” you have found the missing connection.

The reason is:

The starting value is the reference point, so the change is measured against it.

Twenty divided by 80 is 0.25, so the increase is 25%.

Now the calculation has a reason behind it. A rise of 20 is one quarter of 80, the value at the start.

Instead of searching for “percentage change explained” again, your next question can become much smaller:

Why does this step follow from the step before it?

3. Check whether you know the rule but not the condition where it breaks

A correct answer can still hide one more gap.

You may understand the words and the connection, but only in examples where every condition lets the rule work normally.

This is a boundary gap. A boundary is the point where a rule no longer works in the same way.

Keep the percentage-change example, but picture a small online seller checking a simple order sheet. One item had 0 orders in the first period and 20 in the next.

The amount of change is 20.

The usual percentage-change formula compares that change with the starting value. Here that would require dividing 20 by 0, and division by zero is undefined.

So the ordinary formula does not give a normal percentage-change result when the starting value is zero.

The earlier explanation has not become wrong. You have simply found a condition that needs different treatment.

That gives you a third question:

Which condition could I change so that this rule no longer works in its usual form?

You do not need to search for every possible exception.

One clear edge is enough to test whether you understand the rule itself rather than only the pattern of the examples you were shown.

Three different gaps call for three different questions

These checks work best in this order because each one tests a different layer of understanding.

Start with meaning. If a key term is still blurry, the reasoning built on it has nothing firm to stand on.

Then check the connection. If the terms are clear but the bridge between two steps is missing, another definition may not repair the problem. You need to explain why one idea leads to the next.

Last, test the boundary. Once the term and the reasoning are clear, alter one condition and see whether you can still explain what the rule can and cannot do.

These three questions are a practical way to inspect confusion, not a scientific system that covers every learning difficulty. More than one gap can also appear in the same topic.

The useful shift is from one large sentence to three smaller ones:

“Which word is still unclear?”

“What reason connects these steps?”

“What condition makes this rule stop working normally?”

Each question gives you something specific to explain, ask, or test next.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s take one confusing idea and explain its most important word without hiding behind the same term.
  • Let’s place two steps side by side and say the reason that links them.
  • Let’s change one condition in a tiny example and see whether the rule still works in the same way.

You do not need to prove that you understand the entire subject at once.

We are only trying to name the next missing piece clearly enough that you know what to do with it.

When you can name the gap, you no longer have to treat the whole subject as the problem.
