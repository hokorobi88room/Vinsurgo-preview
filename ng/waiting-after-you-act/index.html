You Have Sent It. Now What? 3 Ways to Stop Waiting Like You Still Have Work to Do

Use a simple distinction from Epictetus to know when your part is complete, what you can still act on, and what must now be left to another person.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 01/09/2026 Updated 01/09/2026 Information checked 29/08/2026 7 min read

This article is for you if

You have already done what you can for now, but you keep checking, replaying, or worrying because the answer depends partly on someone else.

By the end, you will be able to

You will be able to mark where your part ends, choose one useful action you can finish, and learn from the eventual result without treating it as a judgment of your whole effort.

The message has left your hands. Why does it still feel as if you have not finished?

You check your phone while getting ready to move on with the evening.

No new message.

You put it away, then find yourself opening the same chat again later.

Maybe you are waiting to hear back after an interview. Maybe a customer has your proposal, a friend has your apology, or a manager has the work you submitted.

The answer matters, so the silence can feel active.

Your mind begins to suggest more work.

Maybe I should send another message.

Maybe I should explain myself again.

Maybe no reply already tells me what they think.

But an unfinished outcome is not always unfinished work.

In Elizabeth Carter’s English translation of Epictetus’s Enchiridion, section 1 begins:

“Some things are in our control and others not.”

The same passage puts “whatever are our own actions” among the things in our control, while things such as reputation and command are not fully ours to direct. You can read the passage in the MIT Internet Classics Archive.

For everyday use, that old distinction gives us a very practical question:

Is there still something here that is actually mine to do?

Let’s stop turning silence into extra work

  • Let’s separate our responsibility from the decision another person has to make.
  • Let’s choose one useful action that can finish on our side.
  • Let’s let the eventual answer teach us without letting it judge everything we did.

1. Find the point where your responsibility ends

Imagine leaving an interview at an office in Lagos and getting home with one difficult question still playing in your head.

You want the offer, but the offer is not one action you can complete by yourself.

Your part includes how you prepared, what you said, whether you promised to send anything later, and what you can improve for another interview.

The employer’s part includes whom else they speak to, what they value, when they decide, and whom they finally choose.

Epictetus’s distinction helps here because it separates your own actions from things that belong partly or fully to other people. In Carter’s translation, our own actions appear on the side of what is “in our control,” while reputation and command do not.

You can make that boundary visible with a simple control line:

Still mine

  • Send the document I said I would send.
  • Note the interview question I could answer better.
  • Prepare for another suitable opportunity.

Not mine

  • The employer’s final decision.
  • The other candidates.
  • The exact hour they reply.
  • Their private opinion of me.

The second side does not contain things you have to stop caring about.

It contains things you cannot do for another person.

Later that night, your laptop may still be open on the table. Instead of checking the inbox again, you write down the one question you want to handle better next time and then close the computer.

Nothing outside you has changed yet.

But you can now see where your work stops.

2. Turn the result you want into one action you can finish

Once you know the boundary, the useful question changes from “How do I make them say yes?” to “What can I complete from my side?”

Suppose you sent a small business in Ibadan a proposal after discussing the work on WhatsApp.

You may want the customer to accept it, but “make the customer accept” is not a task you can finish.

You can check that the proposal says what you mean. You can send any material you promised and make sure the customer knows how to reply.

After that, there may be nothing more to send today.

The same is true after an apology.

You can say clearly what you are sorry for, avoid hiding behind excuses, and correct something that is still possible to correct. You cannot choose the exact moment when the other person is ready to answer.

A short conversion can make this easier:

Result I want:
The customer accepts my proposal.

What needs their decision:
Whether they accept it.

What I can finish:
Check the proposal once, send the promised material, and choose when I will look for a reply.

That last choice gives waiting an edge.

Pick a next check time: a point when new information could actually change what you do.

Until then, reopening the chat does not create a new decision.

The phone can stay face down while you eat, talk with someone at home, take a walk, or start another piece of work.

You are not acting as if the answer is unimportant.

You are recognising that no new action is available yet.

3. When the reply comes, learn from it without making it your whole score

Sooner or later, the answer arrives.

Perhaps a company in Abuja says it is moving forward with another candidate. Perhaps the customer declines your proposal, or the person you apologised to still wants distance.

Now it helps to ask two separate questions.

What happened?

And:

What does this tell me about the part that was mine?

Those questions are not the same.

A rejected proposal can still be clear, accurate, and sent when promised. An accepted proposal can still have been rushed or poorly checked.

So the result matters, but it is not a perfect score for the quality of your action.

Instead, look for information that can improve a future choice.

If several customers ask the same question, your proposal may need clearer wording. If several interviews expose the same missing skill, that pattern may tell you what to practise.

Now picture opening the reply you have been waiting for.

It says no.

The disappointment may be immediate, and there is no need to pretend otherwise.

Then return to the control line: did this reply give you information that changes something you can do?

If yes, you have a new action.

If no, there may be nothing more to solve today.

The answer can hurt without becoming another job.

First mark the boundary, then finish your part, then learn from what returns

These three parts solve different problems, and they work best in that order.

The boundary stops you from taking ownership of another person’s decision. The next action gives your energy somewhere useful to go and a clear point where it can stop.

Then, when the answer arrives, you can use it as information for the next attempt without treating it as a complete judgment of yourself.

If you skip the first step, you may keep sending messages because you still feel responsible for the reply. If you skip the second, you may understand the boundary but still spend the day circling the situation.

And if you refuse to look at the result, you may miss feedback that could improve what you do next.

The aim is not to care less.

It is to care about the answer while being precise about which part is actually yours.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s take one reply we are waiting for and divide the situation into Still mine and Not mine.
  • Let’s finish one useful action from the first side instead of trying to force movement on the second.
  • Let’s decide when new information is worth checking again, then return the time in between to the rest of our day.
