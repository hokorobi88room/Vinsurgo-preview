Before You Trust an AI Answer, Match It With the Right Check

Follow facts back to the source, try working outputs on a small case, and make recommendation reasons visible before you rely on them.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 03/09/2026 Updated 03/09/2026 Information checked 30/08/2026 7 min read

This article is for you if

You use AI for everyday work or learning in Malaysia and want to check the important part without repeating the whole task.

By the end, you will be able to

You can match three kinds of AI output with three checks: trace factual claims, test working outputs, and make the reasons behind recommendations visible.

Someone is waiting for your reply, and the AI answer already looks finished. Can we check the part that could change the outcome without starting again?

A polished answer can create a strange kind of pressure.

When the wording is smooth and the numbers look exact, it is easy to feel that the whole thing has already been checked for you.

But an AI answer can contain different kinds of output at the same time.

A date in a notice is a factual claim. A spreadsheet formula needs to work. A recommendation depends on the reasons used to compare the choices.

Those are three different questions, so they need three different checks.

The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology, or NIST, uses confabulation for confidently stated AI content that is false or wrong. In ordinary words, an AI system can sound certain even when part of the answer is not correct. NIST also identifies over-reliance and automation bias, which means giving too much weight to an automated answer simply because the system produced it. NIST Generative AI Profile

OpenAI’s current ChatGPT guidance makes a similar point. As checked on 30 August 2026, it says ChatGPT can sound confident while being wrong and advises users to verify important facts, quotations, data, technical information, and external references. OpenAI accuracy guidance

So we do not need to turn every AI-assisted task into a full investigation.

We can begin with one smaller question: What kind of result am I about to depend on?

A three-part check

  • Let’s trace a fact that could change what we do.
  • Let’s test an output that has to work before we use it widely.
  • Let’s lay out the reasons when AI says one choice is better than another.

1. When a fact changes your next step, trace it to the source

Imagine that a notice from your condominium management, university, or workplace is shared in a WhatsApp group.

The notice may include Bahasa Melayu and English, so you ask AI for a quick explanation before replying to someone else.

The summary may be useful, but perhaps only two details can change your next step:

  • the date the change starts;
  • the people the notice covers.

Those are the claims worth tracing.

To trace a factual claim means returning to the document or organisation that issued it, rather than checking one summary against another summary.

Open the original notice.

Find the date in the document itself, then find the line that explains who is affected.

If the AI gives you a citation, open that source as well. OpenAI’s current search guidance says search results and citations can be incomplete, outdated, or incorrect, so the source should still be opened when accuracy matters. OpenAI search guidance

Picture the moment before you tap Forward.

The AI summary says the change begins this week, but the original notice gives a later date.

You did not reread every sentence.

You checked the fact that could have sent someone in the wrong direction.

For factual claims, that is the useful habit: follow the consequence back to the original source.

2. When the answer has to work, test a small case you already understand

Now imagine AI has helped you build a spreadsheet for a weekend bazaar stall, club event, or small team record, with amounts entered in ringgit.

Here, finding a source is not enough.

The formula itself needs to behave correctly.

NIST’s Generative AI Profile recommends evaluating claims about AI capabilities with validated methods and reviewing generated code for validity and safety. For an ordinary spreadsheet or calculation, we can apply the same basic idea in a much smaller way: try a case where we already know what should happen. NIST Generative AI Profile

Suppose the formula should add a mark only when a value is above a limit in this made-up example.

Try three simple cases before filling the whole sheet:

Test caseExpected result
Below the limitNo mark
Exactly on the limitNo mark
Above the limitMark added

You know the expected result before the AI formula runs.

If one case fails, you can fix the problem while the sheet is still small.

The same idea works beyond spreadsheets.

If AI gives you a percentage, calculate it once from the original numbers. If it writes code that will rename product photos, test copies of a few unimportant files before touching the real folder.

The aim is not to repeat the whole job.

It is to give a possible error one small, safe place to appear before the result reaches real work.

3. When AI recommends a choice, make its comparison reasons visible

A recommendation creates a third kind of problem.

“Better” is not a fixed fact like a date, and it is not something you can always prove with one test.

Suppose you are helping with a small community event in your taman and ask AI whether a simple spreadsheet or a small web form would be the better way to track registrations.

The AI might choose the web form because it looks more organised.

That may be reasonable, but perhaps the real priorities are different: another volunteer must be able to take over, the setup must stay simple, and the tool only needs to solve this one event.

Here, the useful check is to show the comparison reasons. These are just the points being used to decide why one option is better than the other.

What matters for this eventSpreadsheetWeb form
Can another volunteer take over easily?EasierDepends on setup
Is it quick to prepare?EasierMay take longer
Does it collect the information we actually need?Yes, if kept simpleYes, if set up clearly

Now you can inspect the recommendation instead of accepting the word “better.”

You can see which reasons support the choice and decide whether those reasons match your real situation.

This is where NIST’s warning about automation bias becomes practical. A confident automated recommendation can feel stronger than the reasons underneath it, especially when those reasons stay hidden. NIST Generative AI Profile

So if the AI still says, “Use the web form,” we do not need to ask it to sound more certain.

We can place the reasons beside both options and decide whether the recommendation still holds.

The choice may stay the same or change.

What matters is that you can now see what the judgement is built on.

Different parts of one AI answer may need different checks

The three checks connect because each one answers a different kind of failure.

A factual claim can be true or false, so we trace it to the original source. A formula, calculation, code result, or process can work or fail, so we test it on a small case.

A recommendation is useful only when its reasons fit your situation, so we make those reasons visible before accepting it.

A single AI response may contain all three.

For example, a plan for an event could include a deadline from a notice, a calculation in a budget sheet, and a recommendation about which tool to use.

There is no need to decide that the whole answer is either “trustworthy” or “untrustworthy.”

Separate the parts, then match each part with the check that fits.

That way, AI can still save time without turning a confident-looking answer into automatic proof.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s trace it when it is a fact, or give it a small test when it has to work.
  • Let’s write the reasons beside the options when AI recommends a choice for us.
