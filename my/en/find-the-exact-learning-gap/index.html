When a Lesson Feels Clear Until You Try to Explain It: Find the Missing Piece

Use three checks to see whether the problem is a word you cannot explain, a step you cannot connect, or a rule whose limit you have not found yet.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 31/08/2026 Updated 31/08/2026 Information checked 30/08/2026 7 min read

This article is for you if

You can follow a class, video, or work tutorial while it is open, but later you cannot tell which part is actually unclear.

By the end, you will be able to

You will be able to separate a missing meaning, a missing connection, and a missing limit, then choose a smaller question instead of restarting the whole topic.

It was clear on the screen. Can we find the exact part that vanished when you looked away?

Maybe you watched part of a lesson on the LRT, during a quiet break at college, or at home after work.

While the explanation was open, the steps seemed to fit. Later, when you try to say the idea in your own words, one part no longer comes out clearly.

Sometimes the unclear part is a word. Sometimes the words are fine, but the reason between two steps is missing. Sometimes the rule works in the example, but you do not know the condition that would make it fail.

Those three problems can feel like the same fog.

They need different questions.

Let’s make the confusion smaller before we search again

  • Let’s check whether one important term still has no clear meaning.
  • Let’s check whether the missing part is the reason one step leads to the next.
  • Let’s check whether we know where the rule stops working in the same way.

1. Check whether an important word is still only familiar, not clear

One unclear term can make an entire lesson feel harder than it is.

You may recognise the word from a school slide, a university module, or an office training video. But if someone asks what it means, you may find yourself repeating the same word instead of explaining the idea.

That is a meaning gap: the label is familiar, but the idea behind it is still blurry.

Psychologists Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil called a related effect the illusion of explanatory depth. Put simply, we can feel that our knowledge is detailed until we have to explain how something actually works. Their 2002 experiments dealt with familiar systems and devices rather than online courses, so the finding cannot explain every difficult lesson. What it does support here is narrower: making yourself explain an idea can uncover limits that simple familiarity may hide. (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002)

A useful check is:

Can I explain the important word without using the same word again?

Imagine you are learning percentage change for a maths class, a spreadsheet task, or a simple monthly expense sheet at home.

You can follow the formula on the screen, but “percentage change” still means little more than “the number I get after doing the calculation.”

Try a plain sentence instead:

Percentage change tells me how large a change is compared with where I started.

Now the term has a picture behind it. There is a starting value, a movement, and a comparison between the two.

The whole topic may still need work. But one hidden problem has become visible, which means you no longer need to treat every part of the lesson as equally unclear.

2. Check whether you know both steps but cannot explain the bridge

A different gap appears when the important words already make sense, but the logic between two steps does not.

You understand the first step. You understand the second step. What you cannot explain is why the first one leads to the second.

That is a connection gap.

Research on self-explanation gives us a useful reason to test this kind of gap. Self-explanation means trying to explain the material to yourself while you learn it. In a 1994 study, Michelene Chi and colleagues asked 14 eighth-grade students to explain each line of a text about the circulatory system as they read. Ten students in a comparison group read the same text twice without those prompts. The students who were asked to explain showed a larger gain from the researchers’ pretest to posttest. It was a small study in one subject, so it does not prove that self-explanation always improves learning. It supports the narrower point that producing an explanation can help reveal and build links that reading alone may leave weak. (Chi et al., 1994)

Suppose you are checking a spreadsheet for a class project, a club budget, or a small work report. A value rises from 80 to 100.

The difference is clearly 20. You may also remember that the usual percentage-change method uses 80 as the number underneath that difference.

Then ask:

Why 80?

If the only answer you have is “because that is the formula,” you have found the missing bridge.

The reason is:

We are comparing the size of the change with the value we started from.

So 20 divided by 80 is 0.25. The increase is 25%.

Now the calculation answers a question you can say out loud: the rise of 20 is one quarter of the starting value.

That gives you a much smaller next question than “Can someone teach me percentages again?”

Why does this step follow from the one before it?

3. Check whether you understand the rule but not the condition where it breaks

There is another gap that can hide behind a correct answer.

You know the important words, and you can explain why the steps connect. But all the examples you have seen may use conditions where the rule works normally.

What may still be missing is its boundary. Here, a boundary means the condition where the same rule can no longer be used in the usual way.

Use the same percentage-change idea, but imagine you are testing a spreadsheet before using it for a group assignment, household record, or stock sheet for a small family business.

Suppose a value moves from 0 to 20.

The difference is still easy to see: 20. The problem appears in the next step, because comparing that difference with a starting value of 0 would mean dividing by 0.

Because division by zero is undefined, the usual formula cannot produce an ordinary percentage-change result from a starting value of zero.

The earlier explanation was not wrong. You have reached a condition where the usual rule needs different treatment.

That gives you a third question:

What could I change in this example that would make the rule stop working in the same way?

You do not need to collect every possible exception.

One clear edge can show whether you understand the rule itself or only remember a pattern that happened to work in the first example.

Three questions can turn one big fog into one workable gap

These checks are most useful in this order because each one removes a different kind of uncertainty.

Start with meaning. If an important word is still empty, the later reasoning has nothing firm to stand on.

Then check the connection. If the words are clear but the bridge between two steps is missing, another definition may not solve the problem.

Finally, check the limit. If the meaning and the connection are both clear, changing one condition can show whether you understand the rule beyond the example you were given.

Think of this as a practical way to inspect confusion, not as a scientific classification of every learning problem. More than one gap can also be present at once.

The useful change is simple: “I don’t understand” no longer has to describe the whole lesson.

“Which meaning is unclear?”

“Which connection is missing?”

“Where does this rule stop working in the same way?”

Each question gives you somewhere specific to begin.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s take one idea from a lesson or work task and explain its most important word in plain language.
  • Let’s place two steps beside each other and ask what makes the first lead to the second.
  • Let’s change one condition in a small example and see whether the rule still behaves the same way.

You do not need to prove that you understand the whole subject today.

We are only trying to locate the next missing piece clearly enough to work on it.

Once the gap has a name, the next question becomes easier to ask.
