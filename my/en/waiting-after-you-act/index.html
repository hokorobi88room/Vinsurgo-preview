Waiting for Someone Else's Answer? 3 Ways to Know When Your Part Is Done

A distinction from Epictetus can help you stop treating silence as a task, finish what is still yours, and learn from the reply when it comes.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 01/09/2026 Updated 01/09/2026 Information checked 29/08/2026 6 min read

This article is for you if

You have already sent the message, attended the interview, made the request or apologised, but your attention keeps returning to an answer that now depends partly on someone else.

By the end, you will be able to

You will be able to separate what is still yours to do from what is not, finish one useful next action, and use the eventual result as information rather than a verdict on yourself.

We have already sent it. Why does the waiting still feel like work?

You leave an interview in Kuala Lumpur and glance at your phone before stepping onto the train home.

No new message.

Later, you check again while eating dinner.

Still nothing.

The answer matters, so your mind keeps trying to turn silence into another task.

Maybe I should send one more message.

Maybe I said the wrong thing.

Maybe they have already decided.

But an open situation is not always the same as unfinished work on your side.

In Elizabeth Carter’s English translation of Epictetus’s Enchiridion, section 1 begins:

“Some things are in our control and others not.”

The same passage places “whatever are our own actions” among the things in our control, while reputation and command are listed among things that are not. You can read the passage in the MIT Internet Classics Archive.

For everyday use, that gives us a practical question:

What part of this situation still needs something from me?

Let’s give the waiting a boundary

  • Let’s separate the action that is ours from the answer that belongs to someone else.
  • Let’s choose one next move that can reach a clear ending.
  • Let’s read the final result for useful information without turning it into a verdict on ourselves.

1. Mark where your part ends and the other person’s decision begins

Imagine you have just finished a video interview from your room in Johor Bahru.

You want a good answer, but the thought “I need them to choose me” blends your work with a decision you cannot make.

Your side includes how you prepared, how clearly you answered, whether you promised to send anything afterwards, and what you can improve.

Their side includes whom else they speak with, what the team needs, when they decide, and whom they finally choose.

Epictetus’s distinction is useful because it keeps those two sides separate. It does not say that outside results are unimportant; it says that another person’s action is not your action to perform.

Try drawing a simple control line:

Still mine

  • Send the information I said I would send.
  • Note the question that exposed a weak answer.
  • Prepare a clearer answer before another suitable interview.

Not mine

  • The final choice.
  • Who else is being considered.
  • The exact time the reply arrives.
  • The opinion someone forms after the interview ends.

The second list can still matter to you.

It simply does not become more controllable because you refresh your inbox again.

Picture the ceiling fan turning while your laptop is already closed. You write one line in a notebook about the question you want to answer better next time, then put the notebook away.

The company has not replied.

But your responsibility now has an edge, and waiting no longer has to pretend it is effort.

2. Choose a next move that can finish on your side

Now imagine that the waiting follows a difficult WhatsApp exchange with someone close to you.

You have sent an apology, but there is no reply yet.

You may want the other person to forgive you.

That hope matters, but “make them forgive me” is not an action you can complete.

You can make sure your apology says what you are sorry for. You can repair something practical if it is still possible, and you can stop yourself from sending extra messages only to reduce your own discomfort.

After that, the other person may need time.

The same method can help after you send work to someone else. Imagine you have sent a proposal to a small local client in Penang and are waiting for a decision.

Result I hope for:
The client accepts my proposal.

Part that belongs to them:
Whether they say yes.

Part I can finish:
Check what I promised, send any missing detail, and decide when I will look for a reply again.

That final step creates a next check point. It is simply a later moment when new information could actually change what you do.

Until then, opening email or WhatsApp every few minutes does not create a new useful action.

You might put the phone away while you eat, travel home, or start another piece of work.

You are not acting as if the answer does not matter.

You are stopping the absence of an answer from taking over the whole evening.

3. When the reply arrives, separate the result from the quality of your action

A few days later, imagine reading the answer while sitting at a kopitiam before heading home.

It is not the answer you hoped for.

Now two questions are useful:

What happened?

And:

What does this result actually tell me about the part that was mine?

Those questions do not always have the same answer.

A careful proposal can still be rejected. A proposal can also be accepted even if parts of it were rushed.

So the outcome matters, but it is not a perfect score for how well you acted.

Look instead for information that can improve a future action.

If several people misunderstand the same part of your proposal, that wording may need work. If several interviews reveal the same missing skill, that repeated pattern may show you what to practise next.

There may still be a drop in your stomach when you read no.

Then return to the control line: did this reply give you new information that changes something you can do?

If yes, you now have a new action.

If no, there may be nothing else to solve in this situation today.

The result can still disappoint you.

It does not have to create extra work that was never yours.

First find the boundary, then finish your action, then learn from the answer

These three parts do different jobs, which is why the order matters.

The control line shows where your responsibility stops. A finishable next move keeps uncertainty from spreading across the rest of your day. The final result can then become information for what you try next.

If you skip the first part, you may keep interfering with a decision that is no longer yours. If you skip the second, you may understand the boundary but still spend the evening circling it.

If you refuse to look at the result, you may miss information that could improve your next attempt.

The goal is not to stop caring about the answer.

It is to care without taking ownership of a decision that another person has to make.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s take one answer we are waiting for and divide the situation into Still mine and Not mine.
  • Let’s choose one useful action from the first side that can honestly reach an ending today.
  • Let’s decide when we will check for new information again, then give the time in between back to the rest of our day.
