How to Build Trust With a Colleague Without Forcing Small Talk

You do not need to become more outgoing. Trust can grow when you show that you understood, make the next step clear, and make it easy for either person to correct a misunderstanding.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 28/08/2026 Updated 28/08/2026 Information checked 28/08/2026 7 min read

This article is for you if

You want to build trust with one colleague, but small talk and trying to seem outgoing feel forced.

By the end, you will be able to

You will have a simple way to make working with one person feel clearer and safer without forcing yourself to talk more than you want to.

You want them to trust you, but you do not want to pretend to be more outgoing. Can we build trust another way?

Maybe you see the same person on most working days.

You exchange greetings. You work on the same task from time to time. Nothing is wrong between you.

Then someone else comes in and starts an easy conversation. Within minutes, people are laughing. You may wonder whether trust is built that way, and whether being quiet puts you at a disadvantage.

For this one relationship, let’s try a different route.

You do not need to create more conversation than the work needs. You can make the conversations you already have easier to understand and easier to repair when something goes wrong.

A quiet way to build trust

  • Let’s show what we heard before we act.
  • Let’s make the next step clear so silence does not turn into guessing.
  • Let’s make corrections normal so a small misunderstanding does not become a personal fight.

1. Show what you heard before you act

A colleague explains a task to you.

You listen. You understand. You say, “Okay,” and turn back to your screen.

That may feel perfectly clear from your side. But the other person cannot see what is inside your head.

Did you understand the whole request? Did one detail mean something different to you? Are you both picturing the same result?

You do not need a long reply. You can use a check-back: one short sentence that says what you think you heard.

For example:

“So you want the first section changed, but the second section left as it is. Have I got that right?”

Now the other person can say, “Yes.”

Or they can say, “Almost. Keep the first section too—I only meant the table.”

The cursor is still blinking on the screen. No work has been thrown away. Nobody has to explain later why the finished result looks wrong.

A check-back is most useful when the meaning matters: a deadline, a change to shared work, a request with several parts, or something you are not fully sure about. If the request is already simple and clear, you can leave it alone.

Trust here is not about sounding charming. You are giving the other person a small chance to see that their words reached you accurately.

2. Make the next step clear so they do not have to guess

You can understand a task correctly and still leave another kind of uncertainty.

What happens now?

Suppose a colleague sends you a document that needs changes. You reply, “Sure.”

Two hours pass.

They may not know whether you have started, when they will hear from you, or which parts you plan to change.

You do not need to send messages all afternoon. One clear reply can remove much of the guessing:

“I’ll update the first two sections and send them back this afternoon. I’ll leave the final section unchanged.”

Now the other person has a simple picture of what happens next.

This difference matters because talking more often is not the same as communicating better. A study published in European Management Journal examined remote workers during pandemic restrictions. In that setting, the researchers found that the drop in trust connected with workplace isolation—feeling cut off from people at work—was explained more by poorer communication and information sharing than by how often people communicated. They described good workplace communication as accurate, timely, and useful. (van Zoonen, Sivunen and Blomqvist, European Management Journal)

That study concerned remote work during an unusual period, so it does not prove that every workplace works in exactly the same way. But it gives us a useful distinction: more messages and clearer messages are not the same thing.

That can be freeing if you are quiet.

Picture two quiet afternoons.

In one, your colleague keeps glancing at the message window and wondering whether to ask again.

In the other, they already know what you are changing and when you plan to return.

Both afternoons may contain very little conversation. Only one contains unnecessary guessing.

3. Make it easy to say, “That’s not what I meant”

Now imagine that you finish the work.

Your colleague looks at it and says:

“Actually, that’s not quite what I meant.”

There can be a sharp second of tension.

You listened. You tried to get it right. Their correction may sound like criticism even when it is only information.

You could defend yourself immediately:

“But that’s what you said.”

Or you can keep the door open:

“Got it. I understood that part differently. Thanks for catching it—let’s fix it.”

You are not saying every mistake is yours. You are saying that finding the right meaning matters more than winning a small argument.

Researchers use the term psychological safety for a shared belief in a team that it is safe to take an interpersonal risk. In simpler words, it means feeling able to say something that may leave you exposed, such as admitting that you are unsure. In Amy Edmondson’s study of 51 work teams in one manufacturing company, higher psychological safety was associated with more actions that helped the teams learn. (Edmondson, Administrative Science Quarterly)

That research is about whole teams. One calm sentence between two colleagues cannot create that kind of team climate by itself.

But it gives us a useful picture.

If correcting you usually leads to a defence or an argument, the other person may become more careful about speaking. If a correction can simply lead to, “Okay, I see the difference,” the conversation becomes easier.

And that door works both ways.

A week later, you may be the person who needs to say:

“I might have read this differently from you. Can we check this part?”

The room does not suddenly become more social. There may be no joke and no long conversation.

There are simply two people looking at the same work and making it easier to tell each other what they mean.

Trust becomes easier when neither person has to guess

The three parts work together in order.

First, you show what you heard. That catches different meanings before they turn into wasted work.

Then, you make the next step clear. Once the meaning is shared, the other person does not need extra messages just to know what is happening.

Finally, you make correction easier. If either of you misunderstood something, the relationship does not have to turn the mistake into blame.

Each part removes a different question: Did you understand me? What happens now? What happens if one of us is wrong?

You do not need to remove every silence between you.

You are making the silence easier to live with.

That is different from being the most outgoing person in the room. For two people trying to work well together, it gives trust a practical place to grow.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s start with one colleague you already work with, rather than trying to become more social with everyone.
  • Let’s use one short check-back when the meaning matters: “So what you need is ___ — have I got that right?”
  • Let’s leave room for “No, I meant…” from either person and treat it as useful information we can work with together.
