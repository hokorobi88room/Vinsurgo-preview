Your Part Is Done, but the Answer Is Pending: 3 Ways to Handle the Wait

Use a distinction from Epictetus to see what still belongs to you, finish the next useful action, and stop carrying another person's decision through your day.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 01/09/2026 Updated 01/09/2026 Information checked 29/08/2026 7 min read

This article is for you if

You have already attended the interview, sent the quotation, made the request, apologised, or submitted the work, but your attention keeps returning to an answer you cannot produce yourself.

By the end, you will be able to

You will be able to separate your remaining action from someone else's decision, finish one useful next step, and use the eventual result as information rather than a verdict on your whole effort.

The interview ended hours ago. Why are we still carrying it through the evening?

Imagine you have just left an interview in Nairobi and are making your way home through the evening traffic.

The conversation is over, but it keeps replaying in your head.

You remember one answer that could have been clearer.

You wonder whether they have already chosen somebody else.

Later, your phone lights up and you check it quickly.

No reply.

The answer matters, so the silence can feel like unfinished work.

Your mind starts offering more jobs.

Maybe I should send another message.

Maybe I should explain that answer again.

Maybe no news already means no.

But there is a useful difference between an outcome that is still open and work that is still yours to do.

In Elizabeth Carter’s English translation of Epictetus’s Enchiridion, section 1 begins:

“Some things are in our control and others not.”

The same passage places “whatever are our own actions” among the things in our control, while things such as reputation and command are not fully ours to direct. You can read the passage in the MIT Internet Classics Archive.

For everyday use, we can turn that distinction into one question:

Is there anything here that is still mine to do?

Let’s make the waiting clearer

  • Let’s mark the point where our action ends and another person’s decision begins.
  • Let’s choose one useful next move that can finish on our side.
  • Let’s use the answer, when it arrives, as information without turning it into a judgment of everything we did.

1. Mark the point where your work becomes someone else’s decision

Think again about the interview in Nairobi.

You may badly want the offer, but “get them to hire me” is not one action you can complete.

Your side includes how you prepared, what you said, whether you promised to send anything afterwards, and what you can improve before another interview.

Their side includes who else they speak to, what they value, when they decide, and whom they finally choose.

Epictetus’s distinction helps because it separates our own actions from things that do not belong to us alone. In Carter’s translation, our own actions are listed among the things “in our control,” while reputation and command are not.

You can make the boundary visible with a simple control line:

Still mine

  • Send the document I said I would send.
  • Note the interview question I want to answer better next time.
  • Prepare for another suitable opportunity.

Not mine

  • The employer’s final choice.
  • The other candidates.
  • The exact time they reply.
  • Their private opinion of me.

The second side does not contain things you are supposed to stop caring about.

It contains things you cannot complete for another person.

Picture yourself reaching home after the interview. Instead of reopening your inbox, you write down the difficult question while it is still fresh, then close the laptop.

The employer has not decided in front of you.

But your responsibility is no longer mixed together with theirs.

That boundary keeps waiting from pretending to be work.

2. Give your energy a next step that can actually end

Once the boundary is clear, you can stop asking, “How do I make them say yes?”

A more useful question is, “What can I finish from my side?”

Suppose you have sent a quotation to a small business owner in Kisumu after discussing a piece of work.

You want the customer to accept it, but acceptance belongs to the customer.

You can check that the quotation says what you mean. You can send the material you promised. You can make it clear how the person can come back to you.

After that, there may be no useful reason to send another message today.

The same applies after an apology.

You can say clearly what you are sorry for, avoid hiding behind an excuse, and correct something that is still possible to correct. You cannot choose when the other person feels ready to answer.

A small conversion can help:

Result I want:
The customer accepts my quotation.

What needs their decision:
Whether they accept it.

What I can finish:
Check the quotation once, send the promised material, and decide when I will look for a reply.

That last choice gives waiting a boundary in time as well as responsibility.

Choose a next check time: a point when new information could actually change what you do.

Until then, opening the same chat again does not create a new decision.

Your phone can stay face down while you eat, talk with someone at home, take a walk, or begin another piece of work.

You are not acting as if the answer is unimportant.

You are recognising that there is no new action available yet.

3. When the answer comes, learn from it without making it your whole score

Eventually, the waiting ends.

Perhaps a client in Mombasa tells you they chose another proposal. Perhaps the employer says no, or the person you apologised to still wants distance.

Now it helps to separate two questions.

What happened?

And:

What does this result tell me about the part that was mine?

They are not the same question.

A rejected quotation may still have been clear, accurate, and sent when promised. An accepted one may still have been rushed or poorly checked.

So the result matters, but it is not a perfect score for the quality of your action.

Instead, use it as new information when it can improve something you control.

If several clients misunderstand the same sentence, the wording may need work. If several interviews expose the same missing skill, that pattern may tell you what to practise.

Now picture opening the reply you were waiting for during a break in your day.

It says no.

The disappointment may be immediate, and there is no need to pretend otherwise.

Then return to the control line.

Did this reply give you information that changes something you can do?

If yes, there is a new action.

If no, there may be nothing more to solve today.

The answer can hurt without becoming another job.

First find the boundary, then finish your side, then learn from what comes back

These three parts do different work.

The control line stops you from taking ownership of another person’s decision. The next action gives your effort somewhere real to go and a point where it can stop.

Then the result gives you information only after it exists.

If you skip the boundary, you may keep sending messages because the silence still feels like your responsibility. If you mark the boundary but choose no next move, you may understand the situation and still spend the evening circling it.

And if you refuse to learn from the result, you may miss something useful for the next attempt.

The aim is not to care less.

It is to care about the answer while being precise about which part is actually yours.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s take one answer we are waiting for and divide the situation into Still mine and Not mine.
  • Let’s finish one useful action from the first side instead of trying to force movement on the second.
  • Let’s choose when new information is worth checking again, then return the time in between to the rest of our day.
