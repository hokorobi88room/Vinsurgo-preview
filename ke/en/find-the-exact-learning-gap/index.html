When a Lesson Almost Makes Sense: Find the Gap Instead of Starting Over

Use three checks to see whether the problem is a word, a missing reason, or the edge of a rule, then work on the smallest part that is actually unclear.

Written and edited by vinsurgo editorial team Published 31/08/2026 Updated 31/08/2026 Information checked 30/08/2026 7 min read

This article is for you if

You can follow a lesson while it is open, but later you cannot tell whether the missing part is a term, a reason, or the limit of the rule.

By the end, you will be able to

You will be able to identify a meaning gap, a connection gap, or a boundary gap, then choose one smaller question to work on next.

The lesson felt fine while you were watching it. Can we find the exact place where your own explanation gets stuck?

Maybe you finish a short lesson after class or work, close the video, and look at the notebook beside you.

A moment ago, everything seemed to flow.

Now you try to explain the idea without the teacher, slide, or screen helping you.

One piece is missing.

Perhaps a key word still has no clear meaning. Perhaps you understand two steps but cannot explain the reason between them. Or perhaps the rule works in the example you were shown, but you do not know the condition where it stops working normally.

All three can feel like the same sentence: “I don’t understand.”

They are different gaps, so they need different questions.

Let’s turn one large doubt into a smaller one

  • Let’s find out whether a key word still has no clear meaning in our own words.
  • Let’s find out whether the missing part is the reason that joins two steps.
  • Let’s find out whether we know the condition where the rule no longer works in the usual way.

1. Ask whether the key word has become a real idea in your own words

Sometimes one term makes an entire topic feel harder than it really is.

You might recognise it from a school lesson, a college module, or a short workplace tutorial. Seeing the word again feels familiar, but explaining it without repeating the same word can be much harder.

We can call this a meaning gap: the label is familiar, but the idea underneath is still unclear.

Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil studied a related effect called the illusion of explanatory depth. In ordinary language, people can feel that they understand how something works in more detail than they can actually explain. Their 2002 research examined explanations of familiar systems and devices, not online courses, so it does not mean every learning problem comes from this effect. The narrower lesson is useful: trying to explain an idea can expose a limit that simple familiarity hides. (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002)

Try this small check:

Can I explain the important term without using the term itself?

Imagine you are revising a short maths or data lesson at home after your journey back from school or work. Your phone is beside the notebook, and a class or work group keeps sending messages while you try to finish one idea before moving on.

You can follow the percentage-change formula, but “percentage change” still means little more than “the answer I get from the formula.”

Put it into plain words instead:

Percentage change compares the size of a change with the value at the starting point.

Now the term has a simple picture behind it: where you began, how far the value moved, and how large that movement is compared with the start.

You may still have more to learn. But you have stopped blaming the whole topic for a gap that belongs to one meaning.

2. Ask whether you know the steps but cannot explain the reason between them

A different gap appears when the important words already make sense, but the reasoning breaks in the middle.

You understand the step before the gap. You understand the step after it. What you cannot say is why one leads to the other.

That is a connection gap.

A 1994 study by Michelene Chi and colleagues gives a useful reason to test this kind of break. Fourteen eighth-grade students were prompted to explain each line of a text about the circulatory system to themselves, while ten students in a comparison group read the same text twice without those prompts. The prompted group showed a larger gain from pretest to posttest on the researchers’ measures of understanding. The study was small and focused on one subject, so it does not prove that self-explanation always improves learning. It supports the narrower idea that producing an explanation can help reveal and build links that reading alone may leave weak. (Chi et al., 1994)

Suppose you are helping with a simple contribution sheet for a chama, a student group, or another small community activity. One figure rises from 80 to 100.

You know the increase is 20. You also remember that the usual percentage-change calculation divides 20 by 80.

Now ask the question that copying the formula can hide:

Why 80?

If the only answer is “because that is the formula,” the missing part is not the arithmetic.

The connection is:

The starting value is the reference point, so the size of the change is compared with it.

Twenty divided by 80 is 0.25, so the increase is 25%.

The calculation now answers a clear question. A change of 20 is one quarter of the value you started with.

Instead of searching for another full lesson on percentages, your next question can be much sharper:

Why does this step follow from the one before it?

3. Ask whether you know the rule only in cases where it works normally

You can understand a term and its logic and still have one gap left.

Maybe every example you have seen uses conditions where the rule works without trouble.

The missing piece may be its boundary. Here, a boundary means a condition where the rule cannot be used in the same way.

Keep the percentage-change idea, but imagine you are checking a simple spreadsheet for a kiosk, a small online side business, or a school club activity. One item had 0 orders in the first period and 20 in the next.

The amount of change is 20.

But the usual percentage-change formula compares that change with the starting value. In this case, that would require dividing 20 by 0, and division by zero is undefined.

So the ordinary formula cannot give a normal percentage-change result when the starting value is zero.

The earlier explanation has not suddenly become false. You have found a condition where the normal rule needs different treatment.

That gives us a third question:

What could I change in this example that would make the rule stop working in its usual form?

You do not need to collect every exception.

One clear edge is enough to test whether you understand the rule itself rather than only the examples that happened to work.

Meaning, connection, then boundary: three checks for three different gaps

The order matters because each check looks at a different layer of understanding.

Start with meaning. If a key term is still blurry, the reasoning built on it has no firm base.

Then check the connection. If the terms are clear but the reason between two steps is missing, more definitions may not solve the problem. You need the sentence that explains why one step leads to the next.

Finally, test the boundary. When the meaning and reasoning are clear, changing one condition can show whether you understand the rule or only remember a working example.

This is a practical three-question check, not a scientific classification of every learning difficulty. You can also have more than one gap at the same time.

The useful change is smaller than “understand the whole subject.”

“Which word is still unclear?”

“What reason connects these steps?”

“What condition makes this rule stop working normally?”

Those questions give you something specific to explain, ask, or test next.

Move closer to the person you want to become

  • Let’s take one confusing idea and put its most important term into plain words we could say to another person.
  • Let’s place two steps beside each other and name the reason that connects them.
  • Let’s change one condition in a tiny example and see whether the rule still works in the same way.

You do not need to show that you understand everything at once.

We are only trying to identify the next missing piece clearly enough that the next move becomes obvious.

When the gap becomes smaller, learning can become easier to steer.
